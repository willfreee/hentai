# Chapter 16: The sunk cost fallacy

You've done the math, maybe never out loud, but somewhere in the back of your mind you know the numbers. It has been years of your life, measured in evenings, late nights, and stolen afternoon hours. That adds up to thousands of hours and tens of thousands of sessions, a significant fraction of your total existence spent in front of a screen chasing pixels that never chased you back.

You have a long history with this, and the history has weight. When you imagine a life without the tabs, the nightly ritual, and the familiar cycle of anticipation and crash, a quiet but persistent thought arises:

"I've already come this far. I've spent so much time on this. If I walk away now, what was it all for?"

Or: "This has been part of my life for so long. It must be part of who I am."

Or: "I've explored so much and gone so deep. You can't just undo all of that. It's too late."

Or simply: "I've invested too much to stop now."

In all its variations that thought is the sunk cost fallacy, and it keeps you in a burning building because you paid for the ticket.

Once you see the fallacy clearly, it loses most of its power, so here is what it is. The sunk cost fallacy is a cognitive bias, a systematic error in reasoning. We tend to keep investing in something because we've already invested in it, even when the current and future costs outweigh any reasonable expectation of benefit. The past investment is sunk. It's gone and unrecoverable, out of reach of any decision. But we feel it tugging at us anyway, because we don't want it to have been for nothing, and so we keep going.

The classic examples are almost clichés because everyone recognizes them. You stay in a bad relationship because you've been together five years, as if those years obligated you to waste five more. You finish a terrible book because you've read three hundred pages, as if the suffering so far created a duty to suffer through the rest. You keep pouring money into a failing business because you've already put in so much, as if lost money were a reason to lose more.

In every case the rational decision rests on what happens next. You compare future costs with future benefits. The past is gone and has no vote, and you can't recover it whether you continue or stop. Your decision affects only what gets added to the ledger from this moment forward.

You know this, and you've probably given this exact advice to a friend: "You don't have to finish the book just because you started it." "The years you spent with them don't mean you owe them the rest of your life." It's obvious from outside the situation and confusing only when you're the one who made the investment.

The fallacy has a few signature moves in your mind, specifically where hentai is concerned.

The first is the identity move: "I've been doing this so long that it must be part of who I am." How long you've done something doesn't determine whether it's permanent. You've slept your whole life, but sleeping isn't your identity. You've brushed your teeth for decades without wondering whether stopping would erase a fundamental part of you. Duration tells you nothing about whether you should continue, only how long the habit has been reinforced, and habits, as we've established, can change.

The second is waste aversion: "If I quit now, all those hours were wasted." Those hours are already gone. They aren't in a savings account you can draw on by continuing, and they aren't an investment that pays dividends if you hold the stock long enough. They're behind you and out of reach whether you consume tonight or never again. Quitting doesn't retroactively waste time you've already spent, because that transaction is closed. Quitting affects only whether you spend more time, and the waste worth worrying about is the future waste that continuing guarantees.

The third is the completion gambit: "I've explored so much already, so I might as well see what else is out there." It's like saying you've already walked ten miles into the desert and might as well keep going to see what's on the other side. The exploration has happened, and having explored a lot is no reason to explore more. If anything it's a reason to stop, since you've seen what there is and it hasn't satisfied you yet. More exploration won't redeem what you've done. It will add to the total.

The fourth is the permanence trap: "This is just my sexuality now. You can't undo years of conditioning." You can. Neuroplasticity works both ways, and the brain that learned these patterns can unlearn them. The reward system that desensitized can resensitize, and pathways built through repetition can be dismantled through disuse. You aren't a statue fixed in the shape it was carved. You're a living system that constantly adapts to what you do and don't do. The conditioning is real, and the permanence is an illusion that belongs to the trap.

A deeper layer makes the sunk cost fallacy especially sticky in this domain. Quitting can feel like admitting the whole history was a mistake: that you were wrong about who you are and that a significant part of your life was misspent, with the hours themselves wasted and the meaning you attached to them wasted too. That would take in the justifications you built and the identity you constructed around consumption. If you quit, all of it collapses, and you have to face the possibility that you lived inside a lie for years.

That's terrifying and existentially threatening, and it's much easier to continue and justify the past in retrospect than to stop and face the vertigo of "what was that all for?" This is the sunk cost fallacy in its most personal form, and the resolution is the same. The past doesn't need justifying. It happened and was what it was. You don't have to condemn your history to walk away from it, or declare that everything you did was worthless, that you were a fool, or that your life so far has been a waste. All-or-nothing verdicts like those keep people trapped, because who would accept one? Who wouldn't keep going just to avoid it?

You can hold your history more lightly. You can say: "That was a chapter, and a long one. I did what I did and I was who I was. The trap had me, and now I'm writing a different chapter." You don't have to burn the old pages, only turn them. The question to ask is what you want for the future, and whether the past was valid doesn't enter into it. The past can't be changed by anything you do now. Your current decisions affect only what comes next.

Here is a different way to see your history, as evidence and neither a debt, an investment, nor an identity. You've spent years inside the hentai trap, and that fact proves that the trap works. It has worked on you effectively and consistently for a very long time. Every session you've ever had is a data point confirming one hypothesis: once engaged, this mechanism is extraordinarily hard to disengage from by willpower alone. How long you've stayed shows how well the trap is designed and gives no sign that it's your home.

Consider what your history shows: escalation, tolerance, failed attempts to quit, the post-session void, the brain fog, the lost time, the guilt, the rationalizations, and the broken promises. This reads as a case file and nothing like a résumé, and every piece of it points to the same conclusion. You need to get out, and you need a method that works, because across thousands of trials the trap has shown that willpower isn't enough.

Your history is the most compelling reason to leave. The years you've lost are the argument for losing no more, and each hour you spent in the trap is an hour you now know for certain you don't want to spend again. The past was the trap in operation, and the future can be the trap in ruins. Your history obligates you to learn from it, and what it teaches most clearly is that the trap keeps working as long as you keep engaging with it. You honor the past by ending it, and extending it honors nothing.

Try a thought experiment with two futures. In the first you finish this book and quit. The years in the trap become exactly what they were, years spent in the trap, a long, costly, difficult lesson that finally took hold. You move forward as the fog lifts and your energy returns, and the hours that used to vanish into the screen go to things that matter to you. The past is the past. It doesn't define the present or determine the future, and it's only what happened before you figured it out.

In the second future you don't quit and the sunk cost fallacy wins. You keep going for another year, five years, or ten. The trap keeps operating as it always has, because that's what traps do, and you pile up more sessions, hours, brain fog, and guilt. If you ever look back, the past will be the same. The years you already spent will still be unrecovered and unrecoverable, and the only difference will be how many more years you added.

The past is identical in both futures. It's sunk and can't be changed, so the future is the only variable. In one you stop adding to the loss, and in the other you keep adding. So ask whether the past was worth it, what your history says about you, or whether you can ever get those years back, and the question that matters is how much more you're willing to lose.

You owe the past nothing. It's gone, and it doesn't need you to honor, justify, or redeem it. If you owe anything, you owe it to the future, to the person you'll be tomorrow, next week, and next year, who is waiting to see what you decide. With the fallacy stripped away, the decision is simple. You've already paid enough and don't have to pay more. The ticket you bought years ago doesn't oblige you to stay for the rest of the show. You can stand up and walk out, and the door has been right there all along. The sunk cost was never a chain, only a story about one, and you don't have to believe it anymore.

Here is where that leaves you. The time you've spent on hentai is a sunk cost. You can't recover it, and continuing won't redeem it. Each additional hour in the trap extends the loss and honors nothing. How long you've consumed doesn't prove it's part of your identity, only that the trap is effective.

Your history isn't a debt you owe. It's evidence you can use, showing exactly what the trap does and how it works, and it's the most compelling reason to leave. You don't have to condemn your past to walk away from it, or justify or redeem it. You only have to stop adding to it. The years behind you are gone whether you quit today or consume for another decade, and your current decision affects only the years ahead. The past has no vote and the future is unwritten, and you're ready to write something different.
